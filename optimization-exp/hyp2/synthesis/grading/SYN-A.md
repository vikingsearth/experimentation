## The answer

The mechanical change is small; the missing scaffolding around it is the real work. Across the whole demo there are only about eight places where money is actually compared or divided, roughly fifty monetary literals in `data.py`, and about twenty places where a `$` is hardcoded into an f-string — all of it in five source files. What does not exist at all is the thing multi-currency needs most: there is no money type, no exchange-rate source, no code that ever sums an expense into a total, no way to submit an expense at runtime, and no tests. So the change is best understood as introducing a single `Money` primitive plus a static rate table, normalising every expense to a base currency once at ingress, and then making the eight arithmetic sites consume only the normalised value while the presentation and audit layers carry the original. Expect a couple of days of careful work for someone who has decided the design first; the danger is not that it breaks, it is that a missed conversion silently approves an expense that should have been rejected.

## The decisions that have to be made first

**1. What a money value is.** Options: a bare `{"amount": x, "currency": "USD"}` dict (matches the existing dict-literal style of `data.py`), a `Money` dataclass, or a dependency like `py-moneyed`/`babel`. **Go with a frozen `Money` dataclass holding a `Decimal` and an ISO code, which refuses to add or compare across currencies.** A dict gives you no protection — the whole failure mode here is silent, and a type that raises on a cross-currency operation converts silent wrongness into a loud crash. A dependency breaks the project's stated stdlib-only constraint for a demo that needs perhaps forty lines of code. Note that none of the five reports raised `float` versus `Decimal`; using `Decimal` is my call, not theirs.

**2. Convert once at the boundary, or convert at each comparison.** **Convert once.** Normalise the submitted amount to base currency at ingress, store the original amount, original currency, rate, and base amount on `WorkflowState`, and let every one of the eight arithmetic sites read only the base amount. Per-comparison conversion means eight chances to forget, and the reports already found one call site (`check_receipt_attached`) that would be missed by anyone who only tags `state.expense["amount"]`.

**3. Are policy limits base-currency-only, or defined per currency.** **Base-currency only.** Per-currency limit sets multiply fifty literals by however many currencies you support and create a maintenance problem with no upside for a demo. This decision was explicitly flagged as open by two investigators and it reshapes group B entirely.

**4. Where rates come from and as of when.** Nothing in the codebase has any precedent — all five reports independently reported finding no FX source, table, or utility anywhere. **Use a static hardcoded rate table in `data.py`, in the same style as the other reference data, with an explicit as-of date; pin the rate used onto the state and into the audit trail at submission time.** A live source is out of scope for a stdlib-only demo, and pinning the rate is what makes a run reproducible and an audit trail defensible.

**5. Whether the audit trail carries currency as data or as text.** **Add optional structured amount/currency fields to `AuditEntry` alongside `detail`, and route every `detail` string through the one formatter.** Text-only means the audit trail permanently cannot answer "what currency was that number in" — which is precisely the question an expense audit exists to answer.

**6. What to do with the amounts trapped in prose.** The `$50` per-person and `$30` alcohol caps exist twice: as free-text in `data.py`'s `rules` strings and again as bare literals in `agents.py`. The alcohol check extracts its figure by `notes.split("$")`. **Promote both caps to structured policy fields, generate the human-readable rule text from those fields, and replace the notes-parsing with a structured optional sub-amount field.** Making `notes.split("$")` currency-symbol-aware is a dead end that will keep failing quietly.

## Risks

1. **A missed conversion silently approves something that should be rejected.** The six comparisons and the auto-approve threshold decide outcomes. A 3000 ZAR expense compared against a 3000 USD limit passes. Nothing crashes, nothing logs, the report is approved. This is the whole risk profile of the change in one sentence.
2. **The risk score is distorted rather than broken.** `amount / limit` feeds `min(int(ratio * 30), 40)` — up to 40 of 100 points, and budget utilisation another 15. An unconverted ratio does not produce an obviously silly number, it produces a plausible one that moves the expense across the low/medium/high boundary at 30 and 60 and changes the decision path.
3. **A partial retrofit that tags the expense but not the tool.** The receipt threshold comparison lives *inside* `check_receipt_attached`, not in `PolicyAgent`, which only reads the returned boolean. Anyone who fixes the agent and considers the policy layer done will leave this one wrong, and it will look fixed.
4. **Aggregates that can never carry an original currency.** Spending history and department budgets are rollups. If the accumulation code — which does not exist yet — is written to add bare amounts, cross-currency totals become quietly corrupt, and there is no signal that they are. This is the one place where getting it right later is much harder than getting it right now.
5. **The alcohol check silently disappears for non-dollar notes.** `notes.split("$")` on a note reading "alcohol €25" does not raise; it skips the policy check entirely. A policy hole that reports itself as compliant.
6. **Float precision and rounding.** Conversion introduces multiplication and rounding into a system built entirely on `float`. None of the five reports mentioned this. Half-cent drift in a demo is cosmetic; the same code shape in anything real is not.
7. **The audit trail becomes unreconstructable.** Over a dozen call sites bake `$` into strings before they reach `log()`. Ship the presentation fix incompletely and you have an audit trail that reads as dollars for amounts that were not.
8. **No safety net.** There are no tests, no assertions, nothing. The only documented expectation is the README table asserting four amounts against approve/reject/escalate outcomes — and those outcomes may legitimately change once a sample goes non-USD, so it cannot distinguish a correct change from a regression.
9. **Dependency drift.** The easy answer to "money in Python" is `py-moneyed` or `babel`, which breaks the project's stated zero-dependency constraint. Worth deciding deliberately rather than by import.

## What I would do first

1. **Capture a golden baseline before touching anything.** Run all four sample expenses, save the full stdout including the audit trail. With no test suite, this is the only way to prove that the all-USD path is unchanged. Half an hour, and it makes every step below verifiable.
2. **Write down decisions 1-4** (Money type, convert-at-ingress, base-currency-only limits, static pinned rate table). One page. These reshape the work, and three of them were left explicitly open by the investigators.
3. **Build the `money` module**: `Money` dataclass over `Decimal`, static rate table with as-of date, `convert()`, and the single `format()` helper. No behaviour change, nothing calls it yet.
4. **Convert `data.py`'s six tables** to `Money`, all still base currency, sample expenses still USD. Re-run against the golden baseline — output should be identical. This is the largest volume of edits and the lowest risk, and doing it while behaviour is provably frozen is the point.
5. **Normalise at ingress**: add `currency` to the expense contract, add original/base/rate/as-of fields to `WorkflowState`, default a missing currency to base but log that defaulting loudly. Still USD-only, still byte-identical output.
6. **Fix the eight arithmetic sites** to read only the base amount — and do the `check_receipt_attached` one first, since it is the one most likely to be forgotten. At the same time, promote the `$50` and `$30` literals into structured policy fields and replace the `notes.split("$")` parse.
7. **Only now flip one sample expense to EUR.** Everything above was preparation; this is the first moment behaviour changes, and it changes with the golden baseline available for the other three.
8. **Then presentation**: route all ~20 sites through the formatter, add structured amount/currency to `AuditEntry`, rework the summary table's column widths, update the README table with a currency column and re-verify its expected outcomes.
9. **Last, the missing features** — a CLI input path that accepts amount plus currency, and a first test covering the four samples. Defer the accumulation logic entirely unless it is actually wanted; if it is built, build it converting-then-summing from line one.

## Confidence and gaps

**What was solid.** The inventory is genuinely good and I would act on it. Line-level locations, per-finding confidence tags, and a clean separation between confirmed and inferred claims. Report 3's negative finding — that no code anywhere in `src/` accumulates an expense into a total, verified by exhaustive grep — is the single most valuable item in the set, because it reframes part of the work from "retrofit" to "do not build this wrong." Report 2's catch that the receipt comparison sits inside the tool rather than the agent is the kind of detail that prevents a real bug.

**Where it was thin.** Nobody owned the conversion mechanism itself. All five reports independently reported "no FX source exists, not determined" — which is five confirmations of the same hole and zero proposals for filling it. That is a flaw in how I split the question, not in their work, but the practical consequence is that the reports inventory the cost of the change without pricing its core. Nobody raised `float` versus `Decimal`; report 1 came closest and framed it as a dependency question rather than a correctness one. No one estimated effort, sequenced the work, or flagged that an all-USD golden baseline is the obvious safety net given there are no tests. The counts in my table above are mine — report 1 lists every location but never totals them.

**Overlap and disagreement.** No contradictions, but real duplication: reports 4 and 5 separately enumerate essentially the same set of `$`-hardcoded f-strings in `agents.py`, and three reports touch the alcohol/notes parsing from three angles. Nobody noticed that the `$50` and `$30` caps exist twice — as prose in `data.py` and as literals in `agents.py` — which is a duplication worth fixing and which only shows up when the reports are read side by side.

**Quality defects worth naming.** Two of the five reports open with leftover scratch reasoning rather than their summary — report 1 begins mid-sentence about `tools.py` being a pass-through, report 2 begins "Not monetary comparisons — index bounds and result count." Report 1's "What I could not determine" section ends with a literal "none (beyond the above)" directly contradicting the gap listed immediately above it. Four reports cite paths relative to `general-experimentation/agentic-workflows/` while report 4 uses absolute paths, and none of them sits under the directory this synthesis lives in.

**What I would check before committing.** That the eight arithmetic sites are actually exhaustive — report 2 covered `agents.py` and `tools.py` and report 3 confirmed the two divisions, but nobody ran a comprehensive sweep for comparison operators against monetary names across all five files as a cross-check. That the ~50-literal count in `data.py` is right, since I derived it from locations rather than being given a total. Whether anything outside `src/` reads or writes the monetary structures — report 3 explicitly limited its search and flagged this as unverified. And I would confirm all five investigators read the same tree, given the path inconsistency.
